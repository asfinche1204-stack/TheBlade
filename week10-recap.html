The swing
Every team's worst week and best week, widest first. And a correction.
Last Tuesday this chart was the centerpiece of an argument that wide ranges kill you and narrow ones keep you alive, and it named Off with Their Heads!! — 25.4 points, the narrowest in the league — as the model of how to survive. Seven days later that team scored 162.70 and its range is 69.5, seventh-widest on this page. So the argument needs qualifying. What is still true: four of the five widest ranges belong to teams in the basket, and the narrowest belongs to Gabriel Guglielmo, who is still here. What is not true is that a narrow range is a property of a roster rather than a description of its luck so far. A floor is only a floor until the week it isn't. Collin Allison now owns the widest range of anyone alive — 54.80 to 149.10 — and has survived both ends of it.

The record
162.70, and a retraction

Seven days ago this paper wrote of Off with Their Heads!!: "a range of 25.4 points, the narrowest in the league… It has also never scored 120." The following Sunday it scored 162.70 — more than any team has scored in any week of this season — on a lineup assembled with a three-dollar tight end and a two-dollar defense. The editor regrets the error and intends to keep making it.

Ian Zimmerman is the head at 82.80 — the highest number that has ever gone in this basket, and one that would have survived every other week of the season. Eight remain, with seven weeks left to play.

Week 10 was the richest board this league has produced. The median came in at 117.10, nearly ten points clear of any previous week; four teams cleared 135; the median alone would have finished third on the Week 2 board. And none of that helped Ian Zimmerman, who scored 82.80 seven days after posting 152.60 — the second-best week of the entire season. He finishes fifth of eighteen in season points. Nobody in this league has fallen further between two Sundays — 69.8 points, past the 56.3 Collin Allison dropped between Weeks 1 and 2.

The model, for the first time since September, got it right. It made Survive and Advance its favorite at 32.2 percent on a 78.2 projection, said so in print on this page, and watched it happen. That is two hits in ten tries, which is still barely better than drawing names out of a hat with eight to ten names in it, but the paper is obliged to report it when the thing works as well as when it doesn't.

Now the part this page has to own. Last Tuesday the centerpiece here was a chart called the swing, and the argument was that wide scoring ranges get you killed and narrow ones keep you alive. The exhibit for the defence was Off with Their Heads!! — range of 25.4 points, the narrowest in the league, floor of 93.2, and the line, printed in this column, that "it has also never scored 120." On Sunday it scored 162.70, the highest single week in the history of this league, past the 155.90 dos gatos posted in Week 1. The range is now 69.5 and the chart has been redrawn above with the correction written into it. The underlying point survives — four of the five widest ranges still belong to dead teams — but the stronger version of it does not. A narrow range is not a property a roster has. It is a description of the weeks that have happened so far.

The other thing that happened on Wednesday was that the market stopped. The sheet this desk published last week was the richest of the season by a distance — Josh Allen, Saquon Barkley, Malik Nabers, Trey McBride, fifteen lots, $442 by the house's own numbers — and the room spent $68 on it. Josh Allen went to Jimmy Wheeler for ten dollars. Barkley went to Collin Allison for nothing. McBride went to Gabriel Guglielmo for nothing. Seven of the eleven lots that moved went for zero. The only lot that went above the sheet was Ollie Gordon II at $27, bought by the man who was eliminated six days later.

The chart near the top of this page plots that collapse against the eight sales before it, and the explanation is in the wallet chart lower down: there is no money left. Eight teams hold $207 between them, an average of twenty-six dollars, and one of those teams has nothing at all. The auction phase of this league is finished. What is left is a queue — the waiver order — and Collin Allison just proved how much it is worth by claiming five players for free and posting the second-best score of the week.

Which is awkward, because this page spent two consecutive issues burying the free-claim strategy. Andrew Hunter ran it and died. Ryan Zimmerman ran it and died. Collin Allison ran it on Wednesday — Barkley, Ladd McConkey, Isaiah Likely, Jordan Addison and Carnell Tate, not one dollar between them — and scored 149.10. The honest reading, ten weeks in, is that the free lot was a bad bet when the room still had money and a good one now that it doesn't. The strategy didn't change; the market did.

Then there is Alvin Kamara, who at this point deserves his own column. Skidmark Steve claimed him for nothing in Week 6 and was the head in Week 8. Should Have Used Sleeper claimed him for nothing in Week 8 and was the head in Week 9. Survive and Advance claimed him for nothing last Thursday and was the head on Sunday. Three for three. He goes back into the pool tomorrow at $14 on the sheet, and somebody is going to take him, because somebody always does.

Elsewhere: Brett Kreiser scored 145.80 without spending a dollar and leads the season at 1158.66. Ryan Kelley paid $21 for Malik Nabers, scored 135.80, and is down to four dollars with a 122.0 projection and the safest Week 11 on the board. Eric Allred found Darren Waller and Tank Dell in free agency, scored 107.40 — his best week since Week 4 — and is still at zero. Tony Wetzel's 117.10 was his fourth straight week over 117, and he is also the model's favorite to be the next head, because Week 11 projects him at 65.3.

Ian Zimmerman's fifteen go up tomorrow and the headline is Brock Bowers — the best tight end this pool has offered in a league where half the surviving rosters start a tight end they found in free agency. Drake London, Nico Collins and Bucky Irving are behind him, with Jordan Love back nine days after costing eleven dollars. The sheet says $378. The league has $207. On last week's evidence it will go for about sixty.

Week 11 opens Thursday and the model has two names: Tony Wetzel at 44.3 percent on a 65.3 projection and the editor at 32.7 on 70.2 — the two lowest projections on the board by seventeen points, and 77 percent of the column between them. Both of those are bye-week numbers rather than bad-roster numbers, which the model has no way of knowing and which cost Brett Kreiser nothing in Week 7 when he spent $91 and walked. One of the two has $50 to fix it with. The other has $13. Read it as weather.
